Design philosophy

A world feels alive because it keeps moving without you

Most software is static: open, do the task, leave. Nothing would differ if you came back tomorrow or next Thursday. RUXP is designed as a place that exists while you are gone. This is the lens for every screen, feature, and flow. It is inspired by what Fortnite gets right about live products, and it is not a license to copy Fortnite.

Thesis

A living product has a sense of time, change, history, anticipation, presence, identity, community, surprise, scarcity, ritual, and progression. When it works, players return thinking "wait, what happened while I was gone?" and there is always an answer to "what's happening right now?"

The nineteen principles

  1. Build a world, not just a tool. Ask "what is happening here?", not only "what can the user do?"
  2. Make time matter. Saturday night is not Tuesday morning. The clock is part of the product. No manufactured urgency.
  3. Let the world move without me. Design for return. Absence is not dead time.
  4. Season what benefits from seasons. Chapters that mean a real shift. Never a renamed release.
  5. Change the rules, not only the content. Ship rules instead of features.
  6. Experiment in public. This weekend only. Reversible over permanent.
  7. Give users something to become. Roles, artifacts, history. "This is mine."
  8. Make progress visible. Streaks, records, collections. Never gamify meaningless actions.
  9. Reward showing up. Sparingly, and never punitively.
  10. Create rituals, not just habits. "This is what we do."
  11. Sides and allegiances when natural. Belonging over leaderboards.
  12. Design for conversation. What will one player tell another about today?
  13. Let users bring something back. History should change the future experience.
  14. "You had to be there" moments. Impermanence for experience, never for core function.
  15. Sell identity or belonging, never basic function.
  16. Commerce as experience, never as pressure.
  17. Collaborations change the product mechanically, not cosmetically.
  18. Eventually let other people build. From "we create everything" to "we create the system in which things happen."
  19. Never completely finish. Design anticipation, not chaos.

The Aliveness Test

Run it on every feature, screen, or flow before building. Do not maximize every score; find the opportunity.

Now

1. What is happening right now?
2. What might happen later today?
15. Is there something happening now that won't simply wait forever?

Time

3. What could happen while the user is gone?
4. Does Friday feel any different from Tuesday?
5. Is there anything to anticipate?
12. Could this become a ritual?

Memory

6. Is there anything to remember?
9. Does past participation affect the future?
14. Would someone ever return and say, "wait, what happened?"

People

7. Can users develop an identity here?
8. Can users belong to something?
10. Is there a reason to tell another person about what happened?

Rules

11. Could something temporarily change the rules?
13. Does this create genuine aliveness or merely more notifications?

The cut list

Do not turn the product into a video game. RUXP refuses: daily login rewards; streak-loss nagging; push notifications (none yet); inflated or invented presence; loot, crates, random rewards; paid XP or paid tiers; a permanent rank line on Home; "double XP every weekend" because it dilutes FRIDAY NIGHT; countdown timers on non-events; chat in rooms; arbitrary badges; renaming builds as seasons; making essential functionality temporary.

How every product idea is answered

First, is the core utility good? Then, through the lens: CORE LOOP · RIGHT NOW · WHILE YOU WERE GONE · RITUALS · SEASONS · LIVE MECHANICS · IDENTITY · PROGRESSION · COMMUNITY · "YOU HAD TO BE THERE" · WORLD BUILDING · CUT THE BULLSHIT. Then a prioritized recommendation: the one to three mechanics that transform the product, smallest version first. Never thirty features.
