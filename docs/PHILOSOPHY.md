# RUXP design philosophy: a living product

This is the lens every screen, feature, and flow in RUXP is designed through. It is inspired by what Fortnite gets right about live products. It is not a license to copy Fortnite: no forced XP, battle passes, skins, quests, or gamification where they do not belong. The point is the deeper principles below, used to challenge ordinary static app design.

The condensed version and the checklist live in `CLAUDE.md`; this is the full text.

## Core thesis

Most software is static. A user opens it, performs a task, and leaves. In many apps, absolutely nothing would be different if the user opened the product three hours later, tomorrow, or next Thursday.

We build products that feel alive. A living product has a sense of:

- time
- change
- history
- anticipation
- presence
- identity
- community
- surprise
- scarcity
- ritual
- progression

The product should feel like a place that continues to exist even while the user is gone.

**A world feels alive because it keeps moving without you.**

When appropriate, users return and think: *"Wait, what happened while I was gone?"* And there is regularly an answer to: *"What's happening right now?"*

## Product design principles

### 1. Build a world, not just a tool
Do not only ask "What can the user do?" Also ask "What is happening here?" Think of the product as an environment with its own state, culture, events, history, rhythms, and characters. A tool waits for the user. A world keeps moving. Look for ways to make the product feel inhabited and active without adding meaningless noise.

### 2. Make time matter
Most software treats Saturday night exactly like Tuesday morning. Challenge that. Consider whether the experience should change based on time of day, day of week, weekends, holidays, seasons, cultural moments, product milestones, community activity, or real-world events. Examples: Friday Night mode, Sunday Reset, morning rituals, weekend-only experiences, limited windows, monthly events, live launches. The clock itself can be part of the product. Do not manufacture artificial urgency everywhere. Use temporal mechanics only where they create genuine meaning, fun, usefulness, or community.

### 3. Let the world move without me
When the user returns after being gone, something should occasionally have changed: community activity, new content, temporary rules, events, challenges, discoveries, new participants, milestones, environmental changes, unlocked opportunities. Do not treat the user's absence as dead time. Design for return. Ask: what could happen while the user is away that makes returning interesting?

### 4. Season everything that benefits from seasons
Software does not always need one permanent state. Think in chapters, seasons, eras, campaigns, weekends, events, limited experiments. Instead of merely shipping "Version 4.2", consider whether users should experience "Season 4: Summer Strength" or "Chapter 2: The Expansion". A product can develop history. Users should be able to remember "I was there during that season." Do not rename ordinary releases merely for aesthetics. Seasons should correspond to meaningful shifts in theme, activity, goals, mechanics, or community.

### 5. Change the rules, not only the content
Most product teams add more things. Sometimes it is more interesting to temporarily change how existing things work: double rewards, temporary constraints, alternate modes, community-wide modifiers, new starting conditions, limited permissions, unusual combinations, surprise mechanics. Do not assume every new experience requires a new permanent feature. Sometimes: ship rules instead of features. Temporary rule changes can make a mature product feel new again.

### 6. Experiment in public
Not every idea needs to become permanent infrastructure. Use temporary experiments to explore weird ideas: this weekend only, experimental week, limited beta event, seasonal mechanic, community test. Temporary experiences reduce the cost of experimentation because users understand that the world can change. Prefer reversible experiments over permanent complexity when testing uncertain ideas.

### 7. Give users something to become
Do not treat identity as a profile picture and username. Ask how users can express who they are, what they care about, what they have accomplished, what group they belong to, what style they prefer, where they were, what they experienced. Identity can emerge through roles, collections, achievements, status, customization, affiliations, history, reputation, artifacts. Users should sometimes feel: "This is mine."

### 8. Make progress visible
A product should often help users answer: What have I done? How far have I come? What should I do next? What am I working toward? Progress does not have to mean XP. It can be streaks, milestones, mastery, completion, collections, history, contribution, reputation, discovered items, unlocked capabilities, personal records. Do not gamify meaningless actions. Reward behavior that is genuinely aligned with the purpose of the product.

### 9. Reward showing up
Presence can have value. Consider designing experiences that acknowledge attending live, participating in a moment, joining the community, contributing during an event, returning during a specific window. Not every experience must be permanently available. However, avoid manipulative FOMO. Use "you had to be there" moments sparingly and make them feel special rather than punitive.

### 10. Create rituals, not just habits
A habit is "I do this repeatedly." A ritual is "This is what we do." Rituals have cultural and community meaning. Look for opportunities to create Sunday resets, Friday sessions, monthly events, morning check-ins, launch nights, recurring community challenges, annual traditions. A ritual should feel anticipated. Over time, users should begin organizing around it without needing constant reminders.

### 11. Give people sides, teams, or allegiances when appropriate
People like belonging. Look for natural divisions that can create friendly identity and participation: teams, squads, philosophies, styles, factions, regions, interests. Avoid toxic competition. The goal is belonging + conversation + shared stakes. Not merely leaderboards.

### 12. Design for conversation
A healthy product gives users reasons to talk outside the product. Ask: what will one user tell another user about today? Great prompts for conversation include surprise, discovery, mystery, rarity, events, unexpected outcomes, shared challenges, community accomplishments. If every aspect of the product is completely predictable and individually consumed, consider whether it lacks social energy.

### 13. Let users bring something back
A session should not always disappear when it ends. Ask whether something from today can meaningfully affect tomorrow: collected artifacts, reputation, learning, relationships, unlocked abilities, personal data, earned resources, discovered content, history. User history should not exist only as analytics. When appropriate, history should change the future experience.

### 14. Create "you had to be there" moments
Permanent availability maximizes convenience. It can also reduce significance. Some experiences become meaningful because they happened at a particular moment. Use temporary experiences for celebrations, premieres, community events, launches, seasonal finales, surprise mechanics. Do not make essential functionality temporary. Use impermanence for experience, not punishment.

### 15. Sell identity or belonging, not basic function
When considering monetization, ask whether the premium layer can revolve around expression, status, identity, collection, customization, belonging, enhanced experiences. Avoid unnecessarily locking the core value of the product behind payment. Monetization should reinforce the product's culture rather than fight it.

### 16. Make commerce part of the experience
If the product includes commerce, do not automatically treat the store as a utility page. Ask whether browsing itself can be interesting: rotating selections, themed collections, drops, collaborations, community-driven products, seasonal inventory. Commerce should never become dark-pattern shopping pressure. The goal is discovery and expression.

### 17. Collaborations should change the product
Do not think of partnerships as "put their logo in our app." A meaningful collaboration should introduce something that could not have existed without the partner. Ask: how does this partner temporarily change the world? That could include experiences, challenges, content, rules, rewards, locations, community events, aesthetics. Integrate partners mechanically, not just cosmetically.

### 18. Eventually let other people build
A world becomes dramatically more alive when its creators cannot possibly predict everything inside it. Consider whether users, communities, or partners can eventually create events, challenges, collections, content, groups, spaces, extensions, experiences. Move, when appropriate, from "we create everything" to "we create the system in which things happen."

### 19. Never completely finish
The product should retain some capacity for surprise. Do not design chaos. Do design anticipation. A powerful relationship with a product is not only "I like what this is." It can also be "I want to see what this becomes."

## The Aliveness Test

Whenever a feature, screen, flow, or product concept is designed, evaluate it against these questions:

1. What is happening right now?
2. What might happen later today?
3. What could happen while the user is gone?
4. Does Friday feel any different from Tuesday?
5. Is there anything to anticipate?
6. Is there anything to remember?
7. Can users develop an identity here?
8. Can users belong to something?
9. Does past participation affect the future?
10. Is there a reason to tell another person about what happened?
11. Could something temporarily change the rules?
12. Could this become a ritual?
13. Does this create genuine aliveness or merely more notifications?
14. Would someone ever return and say, "wait, what happened?"
15. Is there something happening now that won't simply wait forever?

Do not try to maximize every score. The purpose is to identify opportunities.

## Important constraint

Do not turn every product into a video game. Avoid shallow gamification such as arbitrary XP, meaningless badges, fake scarcity, endless streak anxiety, manipulative countdown timers, random leaderboards, excessive notifications. Always preserve the product's underlying purpose. The goal is not "make everything Fortnite." The goal is: learn how Fortnite makes a digital product feel alive.

## How to respond to product ideas

Given an app, feature, or product concept: first understand the basic user problem and make sure the core utility is good. Then analyze the concept through the lens above, and specifically identify:

- **CORE LOOP** — the simplest thing the user repeatedly comes here to do
- **RIGHT NOW** — what makes opening the product right now interesting
- **WHILE YOU WERE GONE** — what could meaningfully change between sessions
- **RITUALS** — recurring moments users could anticipate
- **SEASONS** — parts of the experience that could evolve in chapters or seasons
- **LIVE MECHANICS** — temporary rules, events, or experiences that could occur
- **IDENTITY** — how users can express who they are
- **PROGRESSION** — what meaningful history accumulates
- **COMMUNITY** — what gives users shared experiences or something to talk about
- **"YOU HAD TO BE THERE"** — limited experiences that create memories without becoming manipulative
- **WORLD BUILDING** — what would make this feel like a place rather than a collection of screens
- **CUT THE BULLSHIT** — which ideas are gimmicks that should not be added

Finally, give a prioritized recommendation. Prefer the smallest version that creates the largest increase in aliveness. Do not propose 30 features. Find the 1–3 mechanics that transform the product.

The standard:

> something schweet is happening right now.
> some kewl stuff happened while you were gone.
> something else completely new might happen tonight.
